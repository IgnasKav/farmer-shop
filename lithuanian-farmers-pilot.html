Local farms.
Orders that fit the delivery week.

A simple ordering and delivery-planning system for Lithuanian farmers selling directly to households. Publish what you have, say where and when customers can receive it, and get an organised list of orders to fulfil.

Lithuania · Lithuanian storefront3–5 farmersSix live weeksVilnius + Kaunas

Who the pilot serves

Recruit farmers already taking orders through messages or phone calls and running regular deliveries. Use their existing customers to test whether structured ordering reduces coordination work. Include at least one farmer already using a chilled delivery provider.

Begin with Vilnius home delivery and a small number of Kaunas collection stops. Each farmer has a separate store and basket. Agree the pilot price and the paid continuation price during recruitment.

Scheduling rules that make it reliable

  • Give each delivery or collection occurrence its own capacity and status. A farmer can skip a holiday, close a full date, or change one week's window.
  • Validate customer addresses against the configured delivery area; distinguish city coverage from surrounding districts.
  • Reserve stock across all delivery methods so the same produce cannot be sold twice. Availability may be tied to a harvest or fulfilment date.
  • Preserve the date, location, window, and fee agreed on an existing order. Explicit changes require customer notification.
  • Offer only methods compatible with every product in the basket. Chilled, frozen, and ambient products do not automatically share delivery options.
  • Group orders by date, method, and stop; provide product totals for harvesting and packing.

Shipping providers to validate

DPD Fresh is the first candidate. Its Lithuanian service explicitly collects from farmers and transports food requiring 0–4°C. This confirms its relevance, but does not establish that most Lithuanian farmers use it. Source: DPD Fresh.

NNL is an alternative to investigate. It advertises temperature-controlled logistics, individual shipments through full loads, and Baltic coverage. Confirm household delivery arrangements, small-farm pricing, collection minimums, and integration options. Source: NNL transport services.

ShareFreeze could complement collection stops. It offers temperature-controlled food lockers. Confirm relevant locations, compartment availability, pricing, and responsibility for transporting orders to the lockers; do not assume a transport service is included. Source: ShareFreeze.

Pilot implementation: book shipments manually and record tracking references. Before committing to integration, validate farm collection coverage, dispatch and delivery schedules, accepted products, temperature requirements, packaging, minimum volumes, fees, and API access.

Minimum product

Customer experience

  • Lithuanian, mobile-friendly farm pages with products, prices, units, and availability.
  • One farmer per basket and guest checkout.
  • Eligible delivery or collection dates, transparent fees, and order deadlines.
  • A simple payment arrangement per farmer using the selected pilot payment provider.
  • Order confirmations and delivery or collection reminders.

Farmer operations

  • Products, fixed packs, stock, and future availability.
  • Delivery areas, stops, farm slots, and provider dispatch schedules.
  • Cutoffs, capacity, date exceptions, and fees.
  • Packing totals and orders grouped by date, method, and stop.
  • Payment status, cancellations, shortages, agreed substitutions, and refund status.

Later phases: shared baskets across farms, automated route optimisation, subscriptions, variable-weight settlement, multiple courier integrations, and broader geographic expansion.

Run the pilot

  1. Recruit and observe: document one real delivery week for each of 3–5 farmers: products, stock, order deadlines, destinations, payment habits, and exceptions. Record current time spent organising orders.
  2. Configure and verify: turn those actual schedules into store settings and acceptance scenarios. Test a full order-to-collection or delivery cycle, including cutoffs, capacity, shortages, and changes.
  3. Operate for six live weeks: help onboard existing customers, resolve purchase blockers, and review fulfilment and support needs weekly.
  4. Decide on continuation: compare results with the baseline, confirm willingness to pay at the stated price, and prioritise changes supported by observed use.

The parent plan allocates weeks 1–14 to discovery, implementation, and preparation, then weeks 15–20 to live operation. This is a working estimate for two full-time developers, to be reassessed after discovery.

What would make the pilot successful?

Hypothesis: farmers move repeat ordering into the system because it reduces coordination work, and customers can successfully order against real delivery schedules.

  • At least three farmers fulfil orders through the system for four consecutive weeks.
  • At least 100 completed orders across the pilot.
  • At least 30% less order-administration time compared with the initial baseline; track time per order as well as weekly totals to account for changing volume.
  • At least 90% of orders require no manual clarification about delivery or collection.
  • At least three farmers agree to continue at a stated paid price.

These are proposed decision targets, not forecasts. Also track hosting costs, support time per farm, fulfilment failures, and repeat purchases to understand whether the service is economical to operate.
